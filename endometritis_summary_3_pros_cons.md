# Summary 3 — Should I be tested for chronic endometritis (CD138)? The pros and cons in my situation

**My details:** unexplained infertility (semen analysis, ovulation, HyCoSy, hormone bloods, cycle ultrasound all normal); 3 stimulated timed-intercourse cycles + 2 IUI, no pregnancy; never pregnant. **3D ultrasound:** posterior wall slightly thickened, "question mark sign" (kérdőjel-jel) seen, endometrial border fragmented on the posterior wall, otherwise normal. **Bleeding:** one heavy day, 4–5 days total. **GI discomfort worse during menstruation.** No IUD, no PID, no uterine surgery.

**One-line answer:** your ultrasound has moved you from "no indication at all" to "there is a real indication to investigate the uterus" — but the finding it most likely represents is **adenomyosis, not chronic endometritis**, and a CD138 biopsy would be a *separate* question. The pros are now stronger than in Summary 2; the cons are unchanged. The balance tips towards testing **only if the biopsy can be taken during a hysteroscopy you are having anyway, or if you are moving to IVF and want to act on the result** — not as a standalone test to "explain" the infertility.

---

## 1. What your ultrasound description most likely represents

Your three findings map onto named sonographic features used to diagnose **adenomyosis** (MUSA consensus terminology) [21]:

| Your report | Corresponding MUSA feature | Reported accuracy (histology as reference) |
|---|---|---|
| "Posterior wall slightly thickened" | **Asymmetrical myometrial thickening** (an *indirect* sign) | Se 80% / Sp 70% [19] |
| "Endometrial border fragmented on the back wall" | **Interrupted / poorly defined endometrial–myometrial junction (JZ)** (a *direct* sign) | Se 70% / Sp 88% [19]; irregular/interrupted JZ Se 74% / Sp 83% [21] |
| "Question mark sign" | **Question mark sign** (uterine shape/flexion) | Se 41% / Sp 96% / PPV 83% / NPV 77% / accuracy 69% [19] |

- The diagnostic rule used in that validation study — and in MUSA-based practice — is **≥2 features present**. On that rule, transvaginal ultrasound achieved **Se 77%, Sp 96%, PPV 91%, NPV 89%, accuracy 90%** for adenomyosis [19]. **You appear to have three features**, so adenomyosis becomes a plausible (not proven) explanation.
- Pooled literature: **TVUS Se 78.5% / Sp 70.7%; MRI Se 64.8% / Sp 87.5%** — i.e. ultrasound is the first-line test, MRI the tie-breaker [21]. A 2026 prospective study using only direct signs found a combined model with **Sp 93.7% but Se 60.0%** (accuracy 78.6%) — these signs are better at confirming than at excluding [22].
- **Honest caveat:** reproducibility of individual MUSA features is imperfect — inter-rater agreement has been reported as poor, especially for poorly defined lesions [21]. Your report should ideally name the features and, if there is doubt, be repeated or followed by MRI.

**Not all "question mark" uteri have adenomyosis.** A 2026 study gave the sign an objective definition — **uterine corpus flexion angle ≥220°** — and showed that higher flexion angles track the severity of **endometriosis-related pelvic adhesions** (rASRM stage) [27]. So the same appearance is not specific to adenomyosis. That matters because your **menstrual GI discomfort** is also compatible with endometriosis/pelvic adhesions, and VVOG guidance says laparoscopy "could be considered for women in whom pelvic pathology is suspected or those presenting with symptoms suggestive of endometriosis" [18].

**What the ultrasound does *not* tell you:** it says nothing about chronic endometritis. No collected source shows that CE produces a thickened myometrial wall, a fragmented endometrial border, or a question mark sign. **Ultrasound is not a CE test** — CE is diagnosed on tissue (biopsy + CD138) [3].

---

## 2. Do your symptoms fit adenomyosis, CE, or both?

- **Heavy/prolonged bleeding and menstrual pain are the core adenomyosis symptoms.** The JOGC guideline lists heavy menstrual bleeding and pain (dysmenorrhoea, dyspareunia, chronic pelvic pain) — plus infertility — as its presenting problems [24]. A 3D/Doppler–histology correlation study found **heavy periods associated with internal adenomyosis**, and an ill-defined junctional zone on Doppler correlated with symptoms [25].
- **The same symptoms appear on CE's list** — pelvic pain, abnormal/dysfunctional uterine bleeding, and even "mild gastro-intestinal discomfort" — but none of them is specific, and none reached expert consensus as a CE criterion [1,3].
- **Your CE risk-factor profile is low.** The agreed risk factors are IUD use (71% agreement), previous pregnancy loss (81%), caesarean-scar disorder (81%), polyps (87%), adhesions (74%), tubal disease (74%) [3]. You have **none** of these; the only item you have is abnormal uterine bleeding, which is where CE is found in **3–10%** of women investigated [1].


**So:** your symptoms are better explained by the adenomyosis-pattern on ultrasound than by CE — but that does not exclude CE coexisting, because the two conditions travel together (next section).

---

## 3. The case **FOR** testing

**P1. You now have a legitimate "other indication".** ESHRE's reason for not recommending endometrial biopsy was that it should not be done "in the absence of other indications" [15]. Abnormal uterine bleeding **plus** an abnormal uterine ultrasound is exactly such an indication — so the main guideline objection no longer applies to your situation, even though it applied to Summary 2.

**P2. Your pre-test probability of CE is meaningfully higher than baseline.** A 2026 meta-analysis of 10 studies (2,877 women) found **CE in 32% (95% CI 16–53%) of women with adenomyosis**, versus controls: **OR 3.81 (95% CI 2.23–6.50)** [20]. If adenomyosis is confirmed for you, a one-in-three chance of coexisting CE is not a trivial prior.

**P3. There is a plausible biological link.** A 2025 hypothesis paper proposes that a richer endometrial and pelvic microbiota in women with adenomyosis and endometriosis leads to more endometritis and subclinical chronic pelvic infection [29] — *hypothesis-level evidence only*, but it offers a mechanism for the epidemiological association.

**P4. If a hysteroscopy is already being done, the marginal burden is small.** Expert consensus states that the specimen must come from an **endometrial biopsy, not smear or brush** (94% agreement) [3]; taking it during a hysteroscopy performed for your bleeding/cavity assessment adds no new procedure. That is the cheapest way to answer the question.

**P5. CE is treatable, and clearance is associated with better outcomes.** In a 653-patient fertility-centre cohort, "treatment and clearance of CE were associated with higher live birth rates" (persistence was common) [17]; expert consensus supports antibiotic treatment when CE is found in RIF (84% agreement) and RPL (71%) [3].

**P6. There is no non-invasive alternative.** CRP, leukocytosis, leptin and IL-6 do not predict CE [1]; ultrasound is not a CE test; hysteroscopy alone is "insufficient" by consensus (74% agreement) [3]. If you want to know, tissue is the only route.

**P7. Severe CE is not a trivial finding if you move to IVF.** A 2026 meta-analysis (3,190 patients) found untreated CE reduced clinical pregnancy (OR 0.52); in frozen-embryo-transfer subgroups, **severe CE (≥5 CD138+ cells/HPF) was associated with reduced clinical pregnancy (OR 0.26), while mild CE (1–4/HPF) was not (OR 0.94, ns)** [23]. Finding *severe* CE before an IVF cycle is actionable; finding "mild" CE probably is not.

**P8. A negative result is also informative.** It would let you stop looking for an endometrial inflammatory cause and focus on the adenomyosis-pattern and treatment strategy — rather than repeating the test later at a worse time.

---

## 4. The case **AGAINST** testing

**C1. In your actual treatment group (TI/IUI), finding CE has not been shown to matter.** The only study in timed-intercourse/IUI patients found **CE in 54%**, but clinical pregnancy **13.8% vs 10.6%** and live birth **12.5% vs 10.6%** — **no significant association**; the authors explicitly call for larger studies before CE screening has "clinical value during non-ART fertility care" [16].

**C2. You still do not meet the definitions on which the treatment evidence rests.** RIF means failure of repeated **IVF embryo transfers** [1]; only ~2–5% of IVF patients meet true implantation-failure criteria [12]. Your five non-IVF cycles are not RIF, and you have never been pregnant, so the RPL literature does not apply either.

**C3. Low prior probability from your history.** No IUD, no PID, no uterine surgery, no pregnancy loss — four agreed risk factors absent [3]. A positive result is therefore more likely to be a false positive or a borderline "mild" finding.

**C4. The test itself is not standardised.** Thresholds of ≥1, ≥2, ≥5 and ≥10 plasma cells per 10 HPF are all in use [2,3]; reading the *same* tissue with different field selection produced prevalence from **3.1% to 69.5%** [5]; endometrial *epithelial* cells also express CD138, so poor technique causes false positives (81% agreement) [3]; and plasma cells are **focally** distributed, so a blind biopsy can miss them [3]. A borderline result creates a decision problem rather than solving one.

**C5. Adenomyosis explains your symptoms better — and CD138 does nothing for it.** A positive CD138 would not treat adenomyosis; a negative one would not explain your heavy bleeding. The adenomyosis-pattern needs its own plan — the JOGC guideline's symptom-directed options are NSAIDs, tranexamic acid, combined oral contraceptives, LNG-IUS, dienogest/other progestins, GnRH analogues, uterine artery embolisation, and surgery (ablation, excision, hysterectomy) [24].

**C6. Treatment has real costs and harms.** First-line doxycycline (200 mg/day × 14 days) failed in **21.2%** of cases in a large RIF cohort, and multidrug-resistant CE rose **8.3-fold (1.3% → 9.6%)** over 2010–2020 [2]; persistent CE after treatment is common [17,2]; antibiotics also disturb the endometrial/vaginal microbiota that this same literature is trying to characterise [1,2].

**C7. The procedure is not free.** In randomised trials using an endometrial biopsy catheter (Pipelle), **pain and bleeding were significantly higher** than in the control arm [28]; the sample should be timed to the proliferative phase for best yield [3]; and a standalone office biopsy means an extra visit, cost and delay.

**C8. Risk of anchoring and delay.** A positive or borderline result can commit you to repeated antibiotic courses and repeat biopsies ("test of cure") *before* the treatment that actually has evidence behind it. ESHRE's strong first-line recommendation for unexplained infertility remains **IUI with ovarian stimulation**, with IVF "probably not" superior and the choice individualised [15].

**C9. The finding may be clinically irrelevant.** "Mild" CE — 1–4 cells/HPF, or 10–40 cells/10 HPF — showed **no association with outcome** in pooled analyses [23,2]; treating it exposes you to antibiotics for a finding of uncertain meaning.

## 5. Side-by-side

| | **Pro testing** | **Con testing** |
|---|---|---|
| **Indication** | You now have AUB + abnormal imaging = an "other indication" (P1) | Symptoms/imaging are better explained by adenomyosis (C5) |
| **Probability of CE** | 32% if adenomyosis (OR 3.81) (P2) | Risk factors absent; only AUB (C3) |
| **Evidence that it matters** | Severe CE → CPR OR 0.26 in IVF; clearance → higher LBR (P5, P7) | No effect shown in TI/IUI; you are not RIF/RPL (C1, C2) |
| **Test quality** | Only way to diagnose; consensus standard exists (≥5/10 HPF) (P6) | Threshold, counting and staining all unstandardised (C4) |
| **Burden** | Minimal *if* done at an already-planned hysteroscopy (P4) | Pain, bleeding, extra visit/cost if standalone (C7) |
| **Downside of a positive result** | Antibiotics are cheap and mostly effective (P5) | 21.2% resistance, rising MDR, persistent cases, microbiota disruption (C6) |
| **What it changes** | Avoids missing a treatable co-factor before IVF (P7, P8) | May delay IUI/IVF and treat a "mild" finding of unclear meaning (C8, C9) |

**Net:** the decisive question is not "is CE possible?" (it is — roughly one in three if you have adenomyosis) but **"would the result change what I do next?"** If the answer is yes (hysteroscopy already planned, or IVF being planned), the pros dominate. If the answer is no, the cons dominate — because the evidence that treating CE helps a woman having TI/IUI does not exist [16].

## 6. What is likely to be more informative than CD138 — do this first

1. **Get a formal adenomyosis assessment of that ultrasound.** Ask specifically for the **MUSA features** to be reported by name (asymmetrical thickening, interrupted/poorly defined junctional zone, myometrial cysts, hyperechoic islands, linear striations, heterogeneous texture, fan-shaped shadowing, question-mark sign), because the diagnosis rests on **≥2 features** and the report you have is descriptive rather than diagnostic [19,21]. If there is uncertainty, **MRI** is the tie-breaker (specificity ~87.5%) [21,24].
2. **Ask whether the question-mark sign should prompt an endometriosis assessment.** The sign now has an objective definition (uterine corpus flexion angle ≥220°) linked to **endometriosis-related adhesions** [27], and your **menstrual GI symptoms** fit that picture. VVOG guidance: laparoscopy "could be considered… in those presenting with symptoms suggestive of endometriosis" [18]. This is a different question from CD138 and would not be answered by an endometrial biopsy.
3. **Clarify whether a hysteroscopy is actually indicated.** Hysteroscopy examines the *cavity*, not the myometrium — it is **not** the test for adenomyosis [24] — and ESHRE advises against it when imaging is normal [15]. But your imaging is **not** normal and you have abnormal bleeding, so if your clinician proposes hysteroscopy to exclude intracavitary pathology, **that is the moment to add the biopsy** rather than a separate procedure later.
4. **Plan the fertility step at the same time.** ESHRE: IUI with ovarian stimulation is strong first-line; IVF is "probably not" superior, individualised by age, duration, prior treatment and prior pregnancy [15]. If IVF is likely within a few months, that is a rational trigger to do the biopsy once beforehand.
5. **Counsel on prognosis if adenomyosis is confirmed.** In a 2026 cohort of women with junctional-zone adenomyosis having a first single embryo transfer with donor oocytes, live birth was **34.2% vs 50.8%** and miscarriage **32.9% vs 11.1%** versus controls [26] — a reminder that the adenomyosis finding may matter more for your reproductive plan than a CD138 result.

## 7. If you do test — the practical specification

- **Specimen:** endometrial **biopsy** (pipelle or during hysteroscopy) — **not** a smear or brush [3].
- **Timing:** **proliferative phase** (detection is higher than in the secretory phase, 81% agreement [3]; one study sampled days 7–11 [14]). Avoid the late secretory phase, when plasma cells can be confined to the basal layer and missed [1].
- **Stains:** **H&E plus CD138 immunohistochemistry** (CD138: Se 100% vs 75%, Sp 100% vs 65% versus conventional staining) [1,2]; ask about the antibody clone/dilution and the counting protocol, since these change the result [3,5].
- **Threshold to discuss:** consensus favours **≥5 plasma cells/10 HPF (≥0.5/HPF)** (81% agreement) [3]; many IVF studies instead use **≥5 cells per single HPF** for "severe" CE [23]. Ask which convention your laboratory uses.
- **Optional add-on:** **microbial culture** (positive in ~45% of RIF cases; *Streptococcus* 27%, *E. faecalis* 14%, *E. coli* 11%, *U. urealyticum* 11%) or PCR/NGS, to target antibiotics — noting that culture, PCR and histology agree in only ~46–57% of cases [1,6].
- **Interpreting the result:** 0 cells = negative (but focal disease can be missed) [3]; **1–4/HPF or 10–40/10 HPF = "mild", of uncertain significance** [23,2]; **≥5/HPF or ≥50/10 HPF = "severe", the group where outcome associations are seen** [23,2].
- **If positive:** doxycycline 200 mg/day × 14 days is first-line, with **21.2% resistance** reported [2]; consider a **repeat biopsy to confirm clearance**, since clearance was associated with higher live birth rates but persistence is common [17].

---

## 8. Bottom line

**Test if at least one of these is true:**
- a **hysteroscopy is already planned** for your bleeding/cavity assessment (biopsy adds no procedure) [3];
- you are **moving to IVF** within a short horizon and want to identify severe, treatable CE beforehand [23];
- you develop **new discharge, pelvic pain or post-coital bleeding** that suggests active inflammation [1].

**Do not test (yet) if:**
- it would be a **standalone procedure** done purely to "explain" unexplained infertility, with no pre-agreed plan for what a positive or borderline result would change [12,16];
- the goal is to **explain your heavy bleeding or your menstrual GI symptoms** — those are better explained by the adenomyosis/endometriosis-pattern, and CD138 will not treat either [24,27].

**Either way, the higher-yield next step is the abnormal ultrasound, not CD138:** formal MUSA reporting ± MRI [19,21,24], and a decision about whether the question-mark sign and menstrual GI symptoms justify looking for endometriosis [18,27].

# Endometritis — symptoms, mild presentations, and diagnostic options

**An evidence summary built only from the sources listed at the end (all retrieved and read in full text or authoritative full-text guideline form). No claim below goes beyond what those documents state.**

Date compiled: 2026-10-27

---

## 1. Framing: two different diseases share the name

| | **Acute endometritis** | **Chronic endometritis (CE)** |
|---|---|---|
| Histology | Micro-abscess formation and neutrophil invasion of the superficial endometrium, gland lumina and superficial layers [8,3] | Superficial mucosal oedema, increased stromal cell density, unsynchronised epithelial/stromal maturation, and **unusual infiltration of endometrial stromal plasma cells (ESPCs)** [1,2,3] |
| Typical setting | Postpartum, post-abortal, ascending STI-related infection (part of the PID spectrum) [8,9,10] | Incidental finding in infertility, RIF, RPL, AUB; often silent [1,2,3] |
| Dominant symptom | Fever + pelvic pain + uterine tenderness [9,10] | Usually none, or vague/non-specific [1,2,3] |

**"Mild" is not a formal diagnostic category.** In the collected literature the word is used in four distinct ways, and they should not be conflated:

1. **Clinical severity of acute infection** — "mild or moderate" PID/acute endometritis is treated with oral/IM regimens, reserving parenteral therapy and admission for severe disease [8,10].
2. **Presence of only minimal signs** — postpartum endometritis in which "sometimes the only symptom is a low-grade fever" [9]; or PID with "subtle or nonspecific symptoms" or no symptoms at all ("subclinical PID") [10].
3. **Histological grade of CE by plasma-cell density** — e.g. "mild CE" = 10–40 CD138+ ESPCs/10 HPF vs "severe CE" = ≥50/10 HPF in the pooled subgroup analysis cited by Yasuo & Kitaya [2].
4. **"Low-grade" vs "high-grade" CD138 positivity** — a grading scheme used in a 2026 hysteroscopy–histology correlation study (CD138 low-grade 60.0%, high-grade 30.0% of participants) [14].

## 2. Acute endometritis

### 2.1 Symptoms and signs, including mild cases

**Postpartum (the commonest form).** The first symptoms are typically **lower abdominal pain and uterine tenderness, followed by fever — most commonly within the first 24–72 hours postpartum**. Chills, headache, malaise and anorexia are common. **"Sometimes the only symptom is a low-grade fever."** Pallor, tachycardia and leukocytosis usually occur [9].

The scoping review adds a broader symptom list for acute endometritis: **fever, pelvic pain, increased vaginal discharge (abnormal odour, consistency and colour), abdominal pain and distension, abnormal vaginal bleeding, abnormal bowel movements and generalised malaise**. Group A streptococcal endometritis is specifically described as presenting with **pain, diarrhoea and vaginal discharge**, and may progress to sepsis, toxic shock and necrotising fasciitis [8].

**STI/PID-related endometritis.** CDC states plainly that "women with PID often have subtle or nonspecific symptoms or are asymptomatic," that acute PID "is difficult to diagnose because of the considerable variation in symptoms and signs," and that **delay in diagnosis probably contributes to inflammatory sequelae** [10]. CDC also notes that *M. genitalium* "might be associated with milder symptoms" [10]. Because endometritis is one component of the PID spectrum, a mild or even silent endometrial infection is an expected presentation rather than an exception [10].

**Context for "mild":** incidence of postpartum endometritis is about **1% after vaginal delivery, 3% after scheduled caesarean, and 5–10% after unscheduled caesarean** [9] — mild cases are numerically common simply because the denominator is large.


### 2.2 Diagnostic options and when they are recommended

| Option | What it shows | When recommended |
|---|---|---|
| **Clinical diagnosis** (history + temperature + fundal/uterine tenderness) | The basis of diagnosis | **Routinely** — "diagnosis is clinical"; treat on clinical findings (postpartum pain, fundal tenderness, unexplained fever) [9,10] |
| **Endometrial culture** | Aerobic/anaerobic growth | **Rarely indicated**: transcervical specimens are "almost always contaminated by vaginal and cervical flora." Do it **only when endometritis is refractory to routine antibiotics and no other cause is obvious**, using sterile technique with a speculum [9] |
| **Blood cultures** | Bacteraemia | Not routine for uncomplicated cases; obtain when the patient is **immunocompromised, refractory to routine regimens, or findings suggest septicaemia** [9] |
| **Urinalysis + urine culture** | Alternative focus of infection | "Usually done to exclude other causes" [9] |
| **Pelvic ultrasound** | Thickened, heterogeneous endometrium, intrauterine fluid, foci of gas | Helps in postpartum patients with abdominal pain and fever — **but the same findings occur in normal postpartum women** (gas may be visible up to 3 weeks; clots/debris in up to 24%), and **a patient with endometritis may have a normal scan** [8] |
| **CT** | Parametrial extension, abscess; septic pelvic thrombophlebitis (only if clots large) | **If fever persists >48 h (some use 72 h) without a downward trend** on adequate antibiotics — to look for pelvic abscess or septic pelvic thrombophlebitis [9] |
| **Endometrial biopsy for histology** | Neutrophilic endometritis | Not part of the routine postpartum workup. In the PID framework, histopathological endometritis is a **"more specific/definitive" criterion**, and biopsy is **"warranted for women undergoing laparoscopy who do not have visual evidence of salpingitis, because endometritis is the only sign of PID for certain women"** [10] |
| **Laparoscopy** | Salpingitis, other pathology | Used when the diagnosis is uncertain or a surgical emergency must be excluded; "frequently not readily available" and "not easily justifiable when symptoms are mild or vague"; **laparoscopy will not detect endometritis** [10] |

### 2.3 CDC 2021 empirical-treatment thresholds (the operational "when to act" rule)

- **Minimum clinical criteria** — treat presumptively in at-risk women with pelvic/lower abdominal pain and **no other identified cause**, if **one or more** of: **cervical motion tenderness, uterine tenderness, adnexal tenderness** [10].
- **Additional criteria that raise specificity**: oral temperature >38.3 °C; abnormal cervical mucopurulent discharge or cervical friability; abundant WBCs on saline microscopy of vaginal fluid; elevated ESR; elevated CRP; laboratory-documented cervical gonorrhoea or chlamydia [10].
- **Definitive criteria**: histopathological endometritis on endometrial biopsy; TVS/MRI showing thickened fluid-filled tubes ± free pelvic fluid or tubo-ovarian complex, or Doppler evidence of pelvic infection; laparoscopic findings consistent with PID [10].
- Requiring all three minimum criteria before initiating empirical treatment "can result in insufficient sensitivity for a PID diagnosis," and more elaborate evaluation is frequently needed "because incorrect diagnosis and management of PID might cause unnecessary morbidity" [10].

**How well do the clinical signs work?** A 2022 meta-analysis (14 studies, 2,808 participants) found pelvic tenderness sensitivity 0.81 / specificity 0.40; cervical motion tenderness 0.72 / 0.50; adnexal tenderness 0.87 / 0.27, with negative likelihood ratios around 0.4–0.5 — sensitive but poorly specific, and **absence of tenderness does not exclude disease** [11].

## 3. Chronic endometritis

### 3.1 Symptoms, including the "mild"/silent case

- **Expert consensus (2026, 31 international experts, modified e-Delphi):** "CE is generally **asymptomatic or oligosymptomatic with subtle and nondescript gynecologic manifestations**" — 84% agreement. The supporting text states that "the subtle and nondescript gynecologic symptoms in CE are often unnoticed by patients and even by experienced gynecologists," and that **"a quarter of the affected patients lack symptoms"** [3].
- **How uncertain the symptom list is:** in Delphi round 1, *every individual symptom item failed to reach consensus* — vaginal spotting 52% agreement, abdominal discomfort 39%, leukorrhoea 39%, intermenstrual bleeding 61%, prolonged menstrual bleeding 55%. Only after rewording into a single "asymptomatic/oligosymptomatic" statement did consensus (84%) emerge [3]. **This is the key caveat for a "mild case": no symptom or symptom set is agreed to be characteristic.**
- **Narrative review:** CE "is often asymptomatic or present with **non-specific clinical symptoms, such as pelvic pain, dysfunctional uterine bleeding, dyspareunia, vaginal discharge, vaginitis, recurrent cystitis and mild gastro-intestinal discomfort**"; about **25% of patients with CE are asymptomatic** [1].
- **Scoping review:** "CE has **ill-defined symptoms** such as pelvic discomfort, spotting and leucorrhoea"; patients may also complain of hypomenorrhoea, secondary amenorrhoea and infertility; it is "a silent disease usually diagnosed on the workup of secondary amenorrhoea and infertility" [8].
- **No systemic signal:** peripheral inflammatory markers — **CRP, leukocytosis, leptin and IL-6 — do not predict the presence of CE** [1].
- **Prevalence context (i.e. why symptoms cannot guide you):** CE is found in **10–11% of the general population** (hysterectomy series), **3–10% of women with abnormal uterine bleeding**, **up to 72% of women with suspected STI-related PID**, ~**20%** of a 2,190-hysteroscopy series, and as low as **2.8%** in one multicentre IVF cohort — a spread reflecting method and population, not symptoms [1]. A scoping review cites **0.2%–46%** depending on patient profile and biopsy method, and notes "up to 40% of infertile patients" in one comparative study [8,6].

### 3.2 Diagnostic options

| Test | Findings / performance | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Endometrial biopsy + H&E** | Stromal oedema, increased stromal density, asynchronous maturation, plasma cells | Traditional reference standard; plasma cells are hard to identify by morphology alone [1,2] |
| **CD138 (syndecan-1) immunohistochemistry** | **Sensitivity 100% vs 75%, specificity 100% vs 65%, inter-observer 96% vs 68%, intra-observer 93% vs 47%** compared with conventional staining (OR 2.8) [1,2] | **Recommended in addition to conventional histology** [1]; consensus 5B (90%) and 5C (94%: antibody clone/dilution, incubation, section thickness, field number/area must be standardised) [3] |
| **Plasma-cell threshold** | No agreed cut-off; ≥1, ≥2, ≥5 and ≥10 ESPCs/10 HPF all in use. Delphi: **≥5 plasma cells/10 HPF (≥0.5/HPF) "may be appropriate" (81% agreement)**; "≥5/HPF" reached only 58% [3]. Pooled subgroup data: **severe CE ≥50/10 HPF** had worse ongoing-pregnancy/live-birth (OR 0.43) and clinical-pregnancy (OR 0.40) odds than **mild CE 10–40/10 HPF**, whereas **mild CE did not differ from no CE** [2] | Unresolved; flagged as a research priority [1,2,3] |
| **Counting methodology** | In 886 treatment-naïve patients, at a **fixed threshold (≥5 PCs, ×400, 10 HPF)** the field-selection strategy alone shifted prevalence **from 3.1% (random sampling) to 69.5% (independent hotspot), p<0.001**; magnification also changed classification significantly [5] | Measured prevalence is partly an artefact of protocol → standardisation is essential [3,5] |
| **Fluid hysteroscopy** | Five agreed features: **(1) strawberry aspect, (2) focal hyperaemia, (3) haemorrhagic spots, (4) endometrial micropolyps <1 mm, (5) endometrial oedema in the follicular phase** (agreement 97/84/81/94/71%) [3]. Micropolyps correlated with histology in **93.4%** and **86.5%** in older series; hysteroscopy–histology concordance 87% in a RIF cohort [1]. A 2026 latent-class meta-analysis (28 studies): pooled **sensitivity 0.816 (0.728–0.882), specificity 0.806 (0.729–0.871), DOR 18.9** [4] | Useful but **"hysteroscopy alone is insufficient" (74% agreement)** given "substantial incompatibility" with histopathology [3]; the meta-analysis instead calls it "a reliable primary diagnostic tool" [4]. Micropolyps alone: sensitivity 65%, specificity 66%, accuracy 65% vs histologic CE (≥2.5 ESPCs/10 HPF) [2] |
| **Endometrial culture** | Positive in ~45% of RIF cases; *Streptococcus* 27%, *E. faecalis* 14%, *E. coli* 11%, *U. urealyticum* 11%; *C. trachomatis* 2.7%; *N. gonorrhoeae* practically undetectable [1] | Guides antibiotic choice in some protocols; contamination limits it [1,2] |
| **Molecular microbiology (real-time PCR / 16S rRNA NGS)** | PCR panel vs conventional methods: **concordance 46.2% with histology, 58.5% with hysteroscopy, 56.9% with culture** — the methods identify partly different patients [6]. Delphi 5I (77%): NGS/PCR allows comprehensive analysis of culturable and non-culturable pathogens [3] | Adjunctive/emerging; does not replace histology [3,6] |
| **MUM1 (IRF4) IHC** | Nuclear plasma-cell marker; pooled **sensitivity 0.876, specificity 0.853, DOR 40.3**, with very wide credible intervals (few, heterogeneous studies) [7] | Potential adjunct/alternative to CD138; not yet established [7] |
| **Imaging (TVS/MRI)** | No validated CE criteria in the collected sources; imaging is discussed for acute/postpartum disease and for excluding abscess or other pathology [8,9,10] | Not a diagnostic test for CE |

## 4. When are which investigations recommended? (scenario-based)

| Clinical scenario | Recommended first step | Additional/second-line tests | Explicitly *not* recommended routinely |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Postpartum or post-abortal fever with uterine tenderness** | Clinical diagnosis and empirical broad-spectrum IV antibiotics (clindamycin + gentamicin ± ampicillin), continued until afebrile 48 h [9] | Urinalysis/urine culture; blood cultures if immunocompromised, refractory or septic-appearing; endometrial culture only if refractory; CT if fever persists >48–72 h [9] | Routine endometrial and blood cultures; imaging as a primary diagnostic test [9] |
| **Suspected PID (endometritis is part of the spectrum)** | Treat empirically on **minimum criteria** — pelvic/lower abdominal pain + ≥1 of cervical-motion, uterine or adnexal tenderness [10] | Add specificity with temperature >38.3 °C, mucopurulent discharge/friability, WBCs on wet prep, ESR, CRP, gonorrhoea/chlamydia NAAT [10]. **Definitive** tests in selected/uncertain cases: endometrial biopsy (histological endometritis), TVS/MRI, laparoscopy [10] | Withholding treatment because criteria are incomplete; relying on endocervical NAAT alone (negative screening "does not rule out upper genital tract infection") [10] |
| **Infertility, recurrent implantation failure (RIF), recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL)** | Offer **endometrial biopsy with CD138 immunohistochemistry** as part of the evaluation; consensus statement 5A: "endometrial biopsies are recommended to obtain the specimens for histopathologic diagnosis of CE in infertile women" (94%) [3] | Fluid hysteroscopy as an adjunct (five features) [3,4]; culture or PCR/NGS to target antibiotics [1,3,6] | **Routine/universal screening is contested:** ESHRE RIF 2023 states firm recommendations cannot be made but that screening and treating confirmed CE is "acceptable"; CFAS 2020 does not recommend routine testing; a 2026 review concludes routine screening and empirical antibiotics "cannot be recommended" and advises a selective approach [12] |
| **Abnormal uterine bleeding; endometrial polyps; intrauterine adhesions/Asherman; caesarean-scar disorder** | Assess the endometrium at hysteroscopy and take directed biopsy — CE is associated with all of these (consensus agreement 74–87%) [3] | Histologic CE is very frequent with cavity pathology: 85.7% with polyps, 69.0% with submucous fibroids, 78.9% with intrauterine adhesions, 46.2% with septate uterus (≥5 ESPCs/10 HPF) [2] | Assuming CE is the sole explanation — surgical treatment is prioritised when CE coexists with polyps, adhesions or hydrosalpinx (87/87/81%) [3] |
| **Suspected genital tuberculosis (endemic settings)** | Endometrial biopsy for granulomatous inflammation; *M. tuberculosis* causes a chronic granulomatous endometritis subtype with poorly developed caseating granulomata and lymphocyte/plasma-cell infiltrate [3] | Culture/PCR where available [3] | — (TB is described as a major cause of infertility, 40–75%, in high-prevalence areas [1]) |
| **Asymptomatic woman with no reproductive complaint** | No testing | — | Routine CE screening is not supported; over-diagnosis and unnecessary antibiotic exposure are explicit concerns [12,2] |

## 5. Where the guidelines and the evidence disagree

| Source | Position |
|---|---|
| **CDC STI Treatment Guidelines 2021** [10] | Endometritis is one component of PID. Diagnosis is clinical and tiered; endometrial biopsy is a *definitive* criterion in selected cases (notably laparoscopy-negative suspected PID). No routine endometrial culture. |
| **Merck Manual (2024)** [9] | Postpartum endometritis is diagnosed clinically; endometrial cultures rarely indicated; blood cultures for selected cases; image if no defervescence by 48–72 h. |
| **ESHRE RIF guideline 2023** (as summarised in [12]) | "Firm recommendations regarding the diagnostic and therapeutic value of CE cannot yet be established," but screening for CE and treating if confirmed is "acceptable." |
| **CFAS 2020** (as summarised in [12]) | Does **not** recommend routine CE testing in RIF (small, low-quality, heterogeneous studies; no consensus diagnostic criteria). |
| **International expert consensus 2026** [3] | Biopsy + CD138 IHC as the diagnostic backbone; ≥5 ESPCs/10 HPF; hysteroscopy alone insufficient; PCR/NGS adjunctive; treat CE in RIF/RPL. |
| **Latent-class meta-analysis 2026** [4] | Hysteroscopy has good accuracy (Se 81.6%, Sp 80.6%) and is "a reliable primary diagnostic tool" — a more optimistic view than the consensus panel's. |
| **Narrative review 2026** [12] | CE's contribution to RIF may be smaller than assumed; prevalence in RIF may not exceed controls; selective, individualised testing only. |

## 6. Evidence gaps that matter clinically

1. **No universally accepted diagnostic criteria for CE**, and no agreed plasma-cell threshold or counting protocol [1,2,3,5].
2. **Symptom-based diagnosis is not possible** — individual symptoms failed expert consensus (39–61% agreement) [3].
3. **The available tests disagree with each other** — molecular vs histology concordance only 46% [6].
4. **Treatment benefit is plausible but not proven** for all groups; antibiotic resistance is rising — first-line doxycycline resistance 21.2%, multidrug-resistant CE rising from 1.3% to 9.6% over 2010–2020 in one RIF cohort [2].
5. **Over-diagnosis risk** — with a ≥1 ESPC/10 HPF threshold, 49% of samples were "positive" without a live-birth difference; with field-selection variation, prevalence ranged 3.1%–69.5% in the same tissue [2,5].

# Chronic endometritis and the CD138 test — a summary for you

**Your situation:** no cause for infertility has been found in you or your partner (so-called *unexplained infertility*). You have had 3 stimulated cycles of timed intercourse and 2 IUI (intrauterine insemination) cycles without a pregnancy. You have not yet had IVF.

**Short answer:** in your exact situation **no guideline recommends CD138 testing routinely, and no symptom pattern has been validated as a trigger.** CD138 is worth discussing mainly if you have a *separate* reason to look inside the uterus (abnormal bleeding, a polyp or adhesion seen on scan, a history of pelvic infection or pregnancy loss, or you are having a hysteroscopy for another reason) — or if you and your clinician decide to look for chronic endometritis before moving to IVF, accepting that the evidence for that step is weak. Before any CD138 test, the standard "unexplained infertility" checks should be complete — and in your case they presumably are, which is exactly why you have the label.

Everything below comes from the published sources listed at the end; the bracketed numbers refer to those documents only.

---

## 1. What CD138 actually is

CD138 (syndecan-1) is a stain applied to a piece of endometrial lining (an **endometrial biopsy**). It highlights plasma cells. Finding an abnormal number of plasma cells in the endometrial stroma is how **chronic endometritis (CE)** is diagnosed. Four practical consequences:

- It is **not a blood test** and not a scan. Something has to be put into the uterus to get tissue.
- **A smear or brush sample is not adequate** — expert consensus is explicit that biopsy is needed (94% agreement) [3].
- **Timing matters**: detection is better in the **proliferative phase** (first half of the cycle) than the secretory phase (81% agreement) [3]; one 2026 study sampled on days 7–11 [14]. In about 15% of secretory-phase samples the plasma cells sit only in the deep basal layer and are missed if the sample is too shallow [1].
- **The result depends heavily on technique.** With the *same* threshold (≥5 plasma cells, ×400, 10 high-power fields), simply changing *which* fields the pathologist counts moved the measured prevalence from **3.1% to 69.5%** in one cohort of 886 women [5].

## 2. When is CD138 recommended? (what the sources actually say)

| Situation | Is CD138 / endometrial biopsy recommended? | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Repeated IVF implantation failure (RIF)** | **Contested but defensible.** ESHRE's RIF guideline says firm recommendations "cannot yet be established," but that **screening for CE and treating it if confirmed is "acceptable."** The Canadian (CFAS) guideline does **not** recommend routine testing. A 2026 review concludes routine screening and empirical antibiotics **"cannot be recommended"** and advises a selective approach. | [12] |
| **Recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL)** | Supported by expert consensus (antibiotics improve outcomes if CE is found — 71% agreement) and by cohort studies; but the ESHRE RPL guideline (2022 update) **does not mention chronic endometritis at all**. | [3], [13], [1] |
| **Infertility in general** | Expert consensus says endometrial biopsy "is recommended… in infertile women" (94% agreement). **However**, the ESHRE guideline on unexplained infertility gives a *strong* recommendation that **"endometrial biopsy for histological examination is not recommended in the absence of other indications"**. These two statements are in direct tension. | [3] vs [15] |
| **Abnormal uterine bleeding, endometrial polyps, intrauterine adhesions/Asherman, caesarean-scar problems** | **Yes — this is the clearest justification.** CE is strongly associated with all of these (74–87% agreement), and histologic CE is found in 85.7% of women with polyps, 78.9% with adhesions, 69.0% with submucous fibroids, 46.2% with a septate uterus. If a hysteroscopy is being done anyway, taking a biopsy is the natural moment. | [3], [2] |
| **Suspected genital tuberculosis** (high-prevalence countries) | Yes — biopsy shows granulomatous endometritis. | [3] |
| **Failed IUI / failed timed intercourse, unexplained infertility, no symptoms** | **No guideline recommends it, and the only study I found in exactly this group found no benefit in knowing.** | [16], [15] |

**Important: you do not (yet) meet the definition of "recurrent implantation failure" (RIF).** The studies that support CD138 testing in infertility were done mostly in women with RIF — which means repeated failure of **IVF embryo transfers**, e.g. failure after two IVF cycles each transferring at least one good-quality embryo, or after six good-quality embryos across three or more cycles [1]. A 2026 review notes that only about **2–5% of IVF patients** meet criteria consistent with true implantation failure [12]. Because you have not had IVF, none of those definitions apply — so the RIF evidence cannot be transferred directly to you.

The 2026 review puts the general position plainly: **"Routine CE testing may not be justified in all patients with infertility or prior to IVF, particularly when performed solely to increase implantation likelihood, as current data do not demonstrate consistent clinical benefit and may expose patients to unnecessary interventions."** Even in RIF it says testing "should not be performed indiscriminately and is best reserved for selected cases" [12].



### The study closest to your situation

A 2026 prospective study enrolled **50 women undergoing timed intercourse or IUI** (146 cycles), took endometrial samples at hysteroscopy, and diagnosed CE when **≥5 CD138-positive plasma cells** were seen in at least 1 of 10 random high-power fields [16]:

- **CE prevalence: 54%** (27 of 50 women) — i.e. very common in this population.
- **Clinical pregnancy: 13.8% (CE-positive) vs 10.6% (CE-negative)**; live birth/second-trimester ongoing: **12.5% vs 10.6%**.
- **No statistically significant association** between CE (or the number of plasma cells) and pregnancy outcome, in crude or adjusted analyses.
- The authors' conclusion: in this small single-centre cohort CE "was not significantly associated with poorer pregnancy outcomes in TI or IUI treatments," and **"larger studies are needed to determine whether CE screening or treatment has clinical value during non-ART fertility care."**

A second study reports how often CE is actually found depending on why the biopsy was done: **13.1% in "implantation failure," 27.9% in recurrent pregnancy loss, 66.2% after a recent first-trimester loss**, overall 28.5% [17].

## 3. Symptoms: would any symptom (or combination) justify testing?

**Honest answer: no symptom set has been shown to predict CE.** When 31 international experts were asked to endorse specific symptoms, **every single item failed to reach agreement**: vaginal spotting 52%, intermenstrual bleeding 61%, prolonged menstrual bleeding 55%, abdominal discomfort 39%, leukorrhoea 39% [3]. Only the general statement — "generally asymptomatic or oligosymptomatic with subtle and nondescript manifestations" — reached consensus (84%) [3].

So symptoms are a **reason to investigate the uterus**, not a validated trigger for CD138 specifically. From the collected literature, the findings that would make a uterine work-up (and, at that point, a biopsy with CD138) reasonable are:

**Bleeding symptoms**
- Intermenstrual or unscheduled spotting [1,2,3]
- Prolonged menstruation — an independent risk factor in expert consensus (71% agreement) [3]
- Heavy or "atypical" uterine bleeding — CE is found in 3–10% of women investigated for abnormal uterine bleeding [1]; atypical bleeding was an agreed risk factor (74%) [3]
- Dyspareunia [1]; abnormal vaginal bleeding [8]

**Discharge / discomfort**
- Leucorrhoea or altered vaginal discharge [1,8]
- Pelvic pain or pelvic discomfort [1,8]
- Recurrent cystitis and mild gastrointestinal discomfort (listed among the non-specific symptoms) [1]

**History items that raise the prior probability** (expert consensus risk factors) [3]
- Prolonged intrauterine device (IUD) use — 71%
- Previous pregnancy loss / miscarriage — 81%
- Caesarean-scar disorder — 81%
- Endometrial polyps — 87%; intrauterine adhesions/Asherman — 74%; tubal disease (hydrosalpinx, occlusion) — 74%
- Prior pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) — relevant because ESHRE advises counselling women with a PID history about further assessment of the tubes [15]

**What does *not* support testing**
- **Having no symptoms at all.** About **25% of women with CE have no symptoms** [1,3] — so silence proves nothing either way, but it also means there is nothing to react to.
- **A normal pelvic ultrasound.** A normal scan does not exclude CE; conversely, ESHRE states that if 3D ultrasound of the uterine cavity is normal, **no further evaluation is needed** [15].
- **Blood tests.** CRP, white-cell count, leptin and IL-6 **do not predict CE** [1]. There is no blood marker to order first.

**Bottom line:** if you have no bleeding, discharge or pain, there is no symptom-based case for CD138. If you *do* have unexplained spotting, prolonged or heavy bleeding, discharge or pelvic pain, that is a legitimate "other indication" — and under the ESHRE wording, that is exactly the situation in which endometrial assessment is no longer "in the absence of other indications."

## 4. What should be done *before* CD138?

**A. First, confirm that "unexplained infertility" has been properly established.** The ESHRE unexplained-infertility guideline defines it as normal ovarian function, tubes, uterus, cervix and pelvis in a woman ≤40 with adequate coital frequency, plus normal testicular function, anatomy and ejaculate — after at least 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse [15]. The checks that make that diagnosis, and which therefore come *before* any endometrial biopsy, are:

| Check | Recommendation in the guideline | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Semen analysis** | At least one basic analysis per WHO criteria in a quality-controlled laboratory; if below the 5th percentile, repeat after 3 months | [15] |
| **Ovulation** | If cycles are regular (24–38 days, ≤8 days bleeding, cycle-to-cycle variation <7–9 days), **no routine ovulation test is needed**; LH testing, ultrasound monitoring or mid-luteal progesterone only if confirmation is wanted | [15] |
| **Tubal patency** | **HSG or HyCoSy** are both valid; choice depends on clinician/patient preference; Chlamydia antibody testing can risk-stratify | [15] |
| **Uterine cavity** | **Ultrasound, preferably 3D**; MRI is not first-line; if ultrasound is normal, no further evaluation is needed | [15] |
| **TSH** | Considered good practice in pre-conception care; no further thyroid testing if TSH is normal | [15] |
| **Coeliac disease** | May be considered | [15] |
| **Things NOT to do as routine** | Ovarian reserve testing to "explain" the infertility; thrombophilia testing; thyroid antibody/other autoimmune testing; post-coital test; vaginal microbiota testing (research only); routine laparoscopy | [15] |
| **Male add-on tests** | Anti-sperm antibodies, sperm DNA fragmentation, chromatin condensation, sperm aneuploidy, semen microbiology and serum hormones are **not** recommended when the semen analysis is normal | [15] |

**B. Do not use "endometrial biopsy for dating" as a stepping stone.** The same guideline gives a **strong** recommendation against endometrial biopsy for histological examination in the absence of other indications, and states there is no reliable way to diagnose a "luteal phase defect" [15]. CD138 is a different question from dating — but the same logic applies: a biopsy needs a reason.

**C. If a structural cause is suspected, image first, then hysteroscopy.** ESHRE gives a **strong** recommendation that **hysteroscopy to find/correct intrauterine abnormalities not seen on routine imaging is not recommended** in unexplained infertility [15]; the 2025 VVOG guidance adds that a high-quality multicentre RCT found no added value from routine hysteroscopy before IVF/ICSI [18]. VVOG also states: if ultrasound of the cavity is normal, "no further evaluation with hysteroscopy, HyCoSy or MRI is needed" [18]. In other words, hysteroscopy is not a "pre-CD138 screening step" either — it is indicated when there is a finding or a symptom.

**D. Where CD138 fits if you decide to look:** the biopsy is best taken **during a hysteroscopy that is already being done**, or as an office pipelle biopsy in the proliferative phase, and sent for **H&E plus CD138 immunohistochemistry** — CD138 adds substantially (sensitivity 100% vs 75%, specificity 100% vs 65%, better inter- and intra-observer agreement) [1,2].

## 5. If you do have the test — how to read the result

- **Threshold:** the 2026 international consensus says **≥5 plasma cells per 10 high-power fields (≥0.5/field) "may be appropriate"** (81% agreement); a stricter "≥5 per single field" reached only 58%, and ≥1, ≥2, ≥5 and ≥10 per 10 fields are all used in the literature [3,2].
- **"Mild" CE:** in pooled data, **mild CE (10–40 cells/10 HPF) did not differ from having no CE**, whereas severe CE (≥50/10 HPF) was associated with worse ongoing-pregnancy/live-birth odds (OR 0.43) [2]. So a low-count result is of uncertain significance.
- **Don't over-read a positive CD138:** endometrial *epithelial* cells also express CD138, so poor staining technique can create false positives (81% agreement) [3]; a 2026 meta-analysis of an alternative marker (MUM1) exists precisely because of these limitations [7].
- **If CE is confirmed** and you have RIF or RPL, antibiotic treatment is endorsed by consensus (84% and 71% agreement respectively) [3]. First-line is usually doxycycline 200 mg/day for 14 days, but **21.2% of cases were resistant** in one large RIF cohort, and multidrug-resistant CE rose from 1.3% to 9.6% between 2010–2015 and 2015–2020 [2]. Persistent CE after treatment is common [17,2].
- **What is *not* established:** that treating CE improves the chance of pregnancy in women having **timed intercourse or IUI** — that is precisely the open question the 2026 TI/IUI study says needs larger studies [16].

## 6. What I would not do in your position, based on these sources

- **Do not order CD138 as a reflex "add-on" before IVF.** A 2026 review states that "routine CE testing may not be justified in all patients with infertility or prior to IVF, particularly when performed solely to increase implantation likelihood, as current data do not demonstrate consistent clinical benefit and may expose patients to unnecessary interventions" [12].
- **Do not accept a diagnosis based on hysteroscopy alone** — the expert panel says hysteroscopy alone is insufficient (74% agreement) because of "substantial incompatibility" with histology [3], even though a 2026 meta-analysis rates its accuracy as good (sensitivity 81.6%, specificity 80.6%) [4].
- **Do not treat on the basis of a smear/brush sample or a single low-count positive result** without discussing the threshold problem [3,2].
- **Do not let a search for CE delay effective treatment.** ESHRE's strong first-line recommendation for unexplained infertility is **IUI with ovarian stimulation**; IVF is "probably not" superior to IUI (conditional recommendation), and the choice is individualised by age, duration of infertility, previous treatment and previous pregnancy [15]. Your 3 stimulated cycles and 2 IUIs are consistent with that pathway.

## 7. Questions worth asking your clinician

1. Does anything in my history (bleeding pattern, discharge, pain, IUD, previous loss, caesarean scar, PID) count as an "other indication" for an endometrial biopsy — or would this be pure screening?
2. If we test, will the sample be read with **CD138 immunohistochemistry**, and which **threshold** does your laboratory use (≥1, ≥2, ≥5 or ≥10 per 10 HPF)?
3. Will the biopsy be taken in the **proliferative phase**, and can it be combined with a hysteroscopy I am having for another reason?
4. If CE is found: what would you treat with, would you **re-test** afterwards, and what evidence are we relying on given that I have not had IVF?
5. Are we both clear that the evidence for treating CE in women having TI/IUI is not established [16] — and how does that change the decision?
6. Is anything in my work-up *incomplete* per the ESHRE checklist (quality-controlled semen analysis, 3D ultrasound, HSG/HyCoSy, TSH) before we consider uterine investigations [15]?
